# Supplement Marketing Compliance Rulebook (U.S. FTC + FDA)

Compiled October 2026 from ftc.gov and fda.gov primary sources, plus law-firm and trade-press
reports for recent enforcement (marked *[secondary]*). This is not legal advice.

---

## 1. Jurisdiction

| | FDA | FTC |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | **Labeling**: package, inserts, point-of-sale material. In practice FDA also cites websites and social posts that link to a cart (the "commercial bridge"). | **Advertising**: print, TV, infomercials, catalogs, internet, social, influencer, email, reviews, subscriptions |
| Law | FD&C Act as amended by DSHEA (1994); 21 CFR 101.93 (claims); 21 CFR 111 (cGMP) | FTC Act §5 (unfair/deceptive) and §12 (false ads for foods/drugs) |
| Remedies | Warning letters, seizures, injunctions; product treated as an unapproved drug | Orders, consumer refunds, civil penalties, bans, corrective ads |

- No FDA premarket approval for supplements. The company is responsible for safety and for
  substantiating claims.
- FTC liability reaches beyond manufacturers to ad agencies, distributors, retailers,
  catalogers, infomercial producers, affiliates, endorsers and corporate officers.
- FTC gives great deference to FDA on whether a health claim is supported. The two agencies
  usually reach the same conclusion on unqualified health claims.
- FDA reviews video audio (transcripts), images and infographics in warning letters.
  Social media disease claims were cited in at least 5 FDA warning letters in Q2 2025.
  *[secondary]*

## 2. FDA: claim types

### Allowed
1. **Structure/function (S/F) claims.** How a nutrient or ingredient affects normal structure
   or function. Examples: "calcium builds strong bones", "fiber maintains bowel regularity",
   "supports the immune system".
2. **General well-being claims.**
3. **Classical nutrient-deficiency disease claims.** Must disclose how common the deficiency
   is in the U.S. Example: vitamin C and scurvy.
4. **Authorized health claims** (21 CFR 101.14). Require FDA authorization and "significant
   scientific agreement", and must use the authorized wording.
5. **Qualified health claims.** Lower evidence threshold, FDA enforcement discretion, must use
   FDA's exact qualifying language. This is NOT FDA approval.
6. **Nutrient content claims** (21 CFR 101.13, 101.54):
   - "High" / "rich in" / "excellent source" means ≥20% DV per serving.
   - "Good source" means 10–19% DV.
   - "High potency" means ≥100% RDI. For a multi, ≥100% RDI for at least 2/3 of the
     vitamins and minerals present at ≥2% RDI.
   - "Antioxidant" requires a nutrient with an RDI (beta-carotene counts via vitamin A),
     recognized antioxidant activity, and meeting the high, good-source or "more" threshold.
     Every antioxidant must be named.
   - A nutrient with no DV must be stated as an amount ("contains 250 mg of X").
   - Disclosure statement is required if the product exceeds fat 13 g, saturated fat 4 g,
     cholesterol 60 mg or sodium 480 mg.

### Not allowed: disease claims (drugs only)
**Disease** means damage to an organ, part, structure or system so it does not function
properly (e.g. cardiovascular disease), or a state of health leading to that (e.g.
hypertension). Nutrient-deficiency diseases are excluded.

### The 10 disease-claim criteria, 21 CFR 101.93(g)(2)
A statement is a disease claim if it claims or implies any of the following:

1. **Effect on a specific disease or class of diseases.**
   - Not OK: "protective against cancer", "reduces arthritis pain and stiffness",
     "relieves angina", "relief of bronchospasm".
2. **Effect on characteristic signs or symptoms of a disease.**
   - Not OK: "lowers cholesterol", "inhibits platelet aggregation".
   - OK: "helps maintain cholesterol levels already in the normal range",
     "improves absentmindedness", "relieves stress and frustration" (with no disease context).
3. **Abnormal condition associated with a natural state** (pregnancy, menopause, aging,
   menstrual cycle). It counts as a disease if it is uncommon (fewer than half of people at
   that life stage get it) or can cause significant or permanent harm.
   - OK: "mild memory loss associated with aging", "noncystic acne", "mild mood changes,
     cramps and edema associated with the menstrual cycle", "occasional hot flashes
     associated with menopause", morning sickness.
   - Not OK: Alzheimer's or dementia, cystic acne, severe PMS depression (PMDD),
     toxemia of pregnancy, osteoporosis.
4. **Implied through name, formulation, citations, the words "disease" or "diseased", or
   pictures.**
   - Names: "CarpalHealth", "CircuCure", "Arthricure" and "Cold Away" are not OK.
     "Soothing Sleep" is OK only with occasional-sleeplessness context.
   - Formulation: saying it contains aspirin, digoxin, laetrile or other drug ingredients is
     not OK.
   - Citations: a disease-titled study placed prominently is not OK. A balanced bibliography
     that is not over-prominent and genuinely supports the S/F claim is OK.
   - "Disease": "promotes good health and prevents the onset of disease" is not OK.
     "A good diet promotes good health and prevents the onset of disease" is OK.
   - Pictures: abnormal tissue or organs, heart symbols, EKG tracings and sick people are
     not OK. Healthy organs are OK.
5. **Belongs to a class of products that treat disease.** Analgesic, antibiotic,
   antidepressant, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antiviral and vaccine are not OK. "Diuretic that
   relieves temporary water-weight gain", and "laxative" or "anti-inflammatory" in clear S/F
   context, are OK.
6. **Substitute for a disease therapy.** Includes "replaces your medication" and "fewer side
   effects than [drug]". Not OK.
7. **Augments a disease therapy or drug.** Not OK.
8. **Role in the body's response to disease or vectors.**
   - Not OK: "supports the body's ability to resist infection", "supports antiviral
     capabilities".
   - OK: "supports the immune system".
9. **Treats, prevents or mitigates adverse events of a disease therapy.**
   - Not OK: "maintains flora in people on antibiotics".
   - OK: counteracting a drug's depletion of a nutrient.
10. **Catch-all.** Anything else that suggests a disease effect.

### S/F claim mechanics (21 CFR 101.93)
- **Exact disclaimer.** No edits allowed. Use "These statements" for plural claims.
  > This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is
  > not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.
- **Placement.** Immediately adjacent to the claim, OR on the same panel or page in a box,
  linked by a symbol (asterisk).
- **Type.** Bold, at least 1/16 inch.
- **Notification.** Notify FDA within 30 days of first marketing the claim: company, claim
  text, ingredient, and product name.
- **Substantiation.** You must hold evidence that the claim is truthful and not misleading.
- **Possible change** *[secondary, 2026]*: FDA is considering amending the rule to require the
  disclaimer on only one panel. Until it is final, follow the current rule.

### Other FDA requirements
- Label: statement of identity ("dietary supplement"), net quantity, Supplement Facts panel,
  other ingredients, name and place of business.
- Domestic address or phone number on the label for serious adverse event reports.
- cGMP (21 CFR 111).
- New Dietary Ingredient notification (NDIN) for ingredients not marketed before October 15,
  1994.
- Facility registration.
- Drug exclusion clause: an ingredient first approved or substantially investigated as a drug
  can't be a supplement. NMN was reinstated as lawful in September 2025 *[secondary]*.
  CBD is still excluded.
- 2026 FDA priorities *[secondary]*: self-GRAS reform (proposed GRAS rule August 2026) and
  NDIN guidance.

## 3. FTC: identifying claims

- Both **express and implied** claims must be truthful and substantiated. You cannot suggest
  what you couldn't say directly.
- Judge the **net impression** of the whole ad: text, product name, images, depictions,
  audio, context. If an ad has more than one reasonable interpretation, you must substantiate
  every one.
- Copy tests help but aren't required. Obvious implications can be found from the ad alone.
- An S/F claim can **imply a disease claim** through context, and the implied disease claim
  then needs disease-level proof.
  - "Cold Away" plus sneezing people plus "nose and throat health this winter" implies cold
    prevention. Even without the name and images, "winter nose/throat" may still imply it.
  - "Arthricure" plus a before shot with a walker and an after shot dancing implies treatment
    of arthritis.
- Evidence-level and authority claims carry implied claims.
  - "University studies prove" requires those studies, and they must be methodologically
    sound.
  - "90% of cardiologists take it" requires proof of the stat **and** of a heart benefit.
  - "Studied for years abroad" or "subject of U.S. government research" implies a
    substantial body of sound research.
  - "Scientists now agree" requires actual scientific consensus.
- Ads that look like drugs: packaging or names resembling an OTC drug, "ask your pharmacist",
  or "accept no generic substitute" may imply FDA drug approval. A prominent "not evaluated by
  FDA, not an approved drug" disclosure may be needed.
- **Never** say or imply FDA approval or authorization. "Complies with FDA notification
  procedures under DSHEA" is deceptive. "FDA registered facility" can mislead if it implies
  product approval.

## 4. FTC: substantiation

### Standard: "competent and reliable scientific evidence"
This means tests, analyses, research or studies based on the expertise of professionals in the
relevant area, conducted and evaluated objectively by qualified people, using procedures
generally accepted in the profession to yield accurate and reliable results. You must have
it **before** the ad runs.

Under the 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance, health benefit claims generally require
**randomized, controlled human clinical trials (RCTs)**.

The amount of evidence needed depends on these factors (the "Pfizer factors"):
- Type of product. Health products need a high level.
- Type of claim. Claims consumers can't verify themselves, or that are subject to placebo
  effect, need more.
- Benefit of the truthful claim versus the cost and feasibility of research. Expensive
  research is not an excuse for having none.
- Consequences of a false claim. Physical injury, for example someone forgoing proven
  treatment, and economic injury both count.
- What experts in the field consider adequate. Existing government or authoritative
  standards get deference.

### Type of evidence
- Well-controlled human clinical studies are the most reliable. Independent replication adds
  weight. Quality matters more than quantity.
- Epidemiology is acceptable only when RCTs are infeasible (e.g. effects that take decades)
  and experts accept it. It should be supported by a mechanism, and the claim must be
  qualified to match the evidence.
- Animal and in-vitro studies are not enough on their own, especially when human research
  is feasible and is the norm in the field. The 2001 example rejected "absorbs better" based
  on studies of a vitamin-synthesizing animal species and a different in-vitro concentration.
- **Testimonials and anecdotes are never substantiation**, even when genuine.
  Ten true memory-improvement stories with no science is unsubstantiated.

### Quality of evidence
Look for:
- Control group with placebo or sham
- Randomization and double-blinding
- Adequate sample size and duration
- Statistical significance **and** a clinically meaningful effect
- Pre-registered protocol
- Intent-to-treat analysis, with dropouts and compliance reported
- Dose-response
- A biological mechanism
- IRB review

Also:
- No p-hacking, post-hoc subgroups or cherry-picked outcomes.
- **Abstracts are not enough.** Review the full study, with an expert if needed.
- Peer review helps, but publication alone isn't proof. Beware pay-to-publish journals.
- Unpublished or proprietary research and foreign studies are accepted if sound.
- Foreign government approvals (e.g. a German monograph) are not proof by themselves.
  Check the underlying studies and how relevant they are.

### Totality of evidence
- Consider all research, including contrary studies.
- If a stronger body of evidence contradicts the claim, **even a qualified claim is
  deceptive**.
  - Two 6-week studies showing fat loss, against a 12-week study showing none, means
    "substantially reduces body fat" is unsubstantiated.
  - "Proven to aid weight loss" based on fiber studies with different proportions, when
    numerous studies at the product's dose show no effect, is deceptive even if qualified.
- Positive example: a double-blind, placebo-controlled study on the product, published,
  plus European trials at a similar formula and dose that were reviewed, likely substantiates
  a "joint flexibility" claim.

### Relevance to the product and claim
- **Formula, dose and form must match.** A different extraction method gives a different
  chemical profile, so the clinical trials don't transfer.
- **Combinations.** Studies on single ingredients don't prove a blend's efficacy or safety.
  For example, two stimulant herbs together carry cumulative risk.
- **Population must match.** Studies in people with impaired brain circulation don't support
  memory claims for healthy adults.
- **Food versus nutrient.** Epidemiology on foods high in X doesn't prove a supplement of
  isolated X works, if feasible direct research hasn't been done.
- Don't exaggerate the extent, nature or permanence of study effects, or imply more
  certainty than exists.
- Superiority claims ("absorbs better", "more effective than…") need head-to-head human
  evidence if feasible and expected in the field.

## 5. FTC: disclosures and qualifications

- **Section 15 duty to disclose** material information.
  - Limited applicability: if only 2% of the target audience has the deficiency behind a
    "fights fatigue" claim, disclose that.
  - Study conditions: if weight loss occurred alongside diet and exercise, say users must do
    the same.
  - Safety risks: disclose when the ad makes safety claims ("without side effects of OTC pain
    relievers" when it causes nausea). Disclose **even without** safety claims when the risk
    is serious, e.g. an ingredient that raises blood pressure.
- **Clear and conspicuous** means:
  - Easily noticed and easily understood, in plain language
  - Close to the claim
  - Size, contrast and duration consumers will catch
  - Both audio and visual when the claim is in both
  - Unavoidable online: not behind hyperlinks, hover text or "see terms"
  - Not undercut by contradictory elements
- **These don't count:** fine print at the bottom, a disclaimer buried in body copy, a brief
  video superscript, or an easily missed web disclaimer.
  - "LOSE 5 LBS IN 10 DAYS" plus "just take 3x a day" plus before/after photos plus fine-print
    "diet and exercise required" is deceptive. Remove the product-alone implication and
    disclose prominently. If the product adds nothing beyond diet and exercise, you can't
    advertise it for weight loss at all.
- **Vague qualifiers fail.** "May", "helps", "promising" and "preliminary" don't convey
  limited science. For emerging science, state the limits plainly and say more research is
  needed to confirm. Never make qualified claims against a stronger contrary body of evidence.
- **The DSHEA disclaimer in ads.** DSHEA doesn't require it in advertising, but it may be
  needed to prevent an impression of FDA review. It **never cures** deceptive or disease
  claims: an ad saying "treats diabetes" with a prominent DSHEA disclaimer is still
  deceptive.

## 6. Endorsements, testimonials, influencers (Endorsement Guides 16 CFR 255, updated 2023)

- An endorsement can't convey a claim the advertiser couldn't make directly. Honest opinion
  isn't enough; scientific backing is still required.
- **Testimonials and typical results.** Either have proof the result is representative, or
  clearly disclose generally expected results.
  - "Results may vary" and "results not typical" alone are insufficient.
  - Example: a "lost 20 lbs in 8 weeks" testimonial when the study average was 6 lbs needs,
    next to the quote at equal size, something like "Average weight loss in clinical study
    was 6 lbs."
- **Expert endorsers** must:
  - Have real, relevant qualifications that aren't overstated. A physical therapist
    presented as "Doctor" is deceptive.
  - Have actually evaluated the product as an expert would.
  - Have any material connection disclosed, e.g. being a paid company officer.
- **Material connection** is anything a significant minority of consumers wouldn't expect
  that affects credibility: payment, free product, commission, employment, family, equity.
  - A paid book author naming the brand, then advertised as "the only brand recommended in
    this best-seller", is deceptive.
- **Disclosure wording.**
  - Good: "#ad" or "Ad" at the start, "Paid partnership with [Brand]", "Sponsored by
    [Brand]", "[Brand] gave me this free", "Paid link".
  - Bad: "#sp", "#spon", "#collab", "#ambassador", "#gifted" alone, "#thanks[Brand]",
    "#sweeps", "affiliate link" alone, a discount code alone, or hashtags buried among others.
- **Disclosure placement.**
  - In the video itself, both on screen and spoken, at the start. The description alone is
    not enough.
  - Before the caption's "more" cutoff.
  - Superimposed with contrast on Stories.
  - Repeated during livestreams.
  - In **every** post.
  - Not only in a profile bio or a comment.
  - Platform paid-partnership tools may not be sufficient by themselves.
- **Brand duties.** Train endorsers on claims and disclosures, monitor them, and correct
  violations. Expect stricter programs for health products. Using an agency doesn't shift
  liability.
- Ordinary customers with no incentive need no disclosure. Any incentive (free product,
  contest entry, discount) does need one.

## 7. Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (16 CFR 465, effective October 21, 2024)

Civil penalties are about $53k per violation, adjusted yearly. Prohibited:
- Fake reviews or testimonials, including AI-generated ones and ones misrepresenting a real
  person's experience. Also buying or selling them.
- Incentives conditioned, expressly or implicitly, on positive (or negative) sentiment.
- Insider reviews (officers, managers, employees, agents, relatives) without disclosure.
  Soliciting such reviews without disclosure is also prohibited.
- Company-controlled "independent" review sites.
- Suppressing reviews through unfounded legal threats, intimidation or false accusations.
- Misrepresenting that displayed reviews are all or most reviews when negatives are hidden.
- Selling or buying fake social influence indicators (followers, views, likes).

Also deceptive under the Endorsement Guides: review gating (asking only happy customers),
suppressing or delaying only negative reviews, and pinning 5-star reviews in a misleading way.

Acceptable: delaying all reviews equally, contacting unhappy customers, asking for updates.

Enforcement example: TruHeight (April 2026) used employee-written reviews and free product or
discounts for 5-star reviews, alongside "clinically proven" growth claims for kids.
*[secondary]*

## 8. Traditional-use claims

- Either substantiate with science, or make clear that the sole basis is history of use.
- Prominently disclose that efficacy hasn't been confirmed by research.
  - "Ancient Native American remedy to aid digestion" needs a prominent no-science disclaimer
    if it implies proof.
  - The TCM "mental clarity" example with a clear "not confirmed by research" statement is
    adequate.
- Document the actual historical use and don't overstate it.
- The **product must match** the traditional form, formula and dose. A concentrated extract
  capsule is not the same as a traditional raw tea.
- **Never** use traditional use for disease claims, even qualified. A pioneer folk remedy
  "for shrinking tumors" can't be run.
- Don't undercut the disclaimer with testimonials or efficacy imagery.

## 9. Third-party literature (DSHEA §5)

Articles, books and abstracts used in sales are exempt from labeling only if all of these hold:
- Reprinted in their entirety
- Not false or misleading
- Don't promote a specific brand
- Presented with balanced material
- Physically separate from the product

When a marketer quotes or excerpts literature in its ads (e.g. a "Miracle Cancer Cure" book),
that becomes the marketer's advertising claim and must be substantiated.

## 10. Weight loss: FTC "Gut Check"

These 7 claims are always false. Never run them:
1. Lose 2+ lbs a week for a month or more without diet or exercise
2. Substantial weight loss no matter what or how much you eat
3. Permanent weight loss after stopping the product
4. Blocks fat or calorie absorption enough to cause substantial weight loss
5. Safely lose more than 3 lbs a week for more than 4 weeks
6. Substantial weight loss for all users
7. Substantial weight loss from something worn on the body or rubbed into the skin

## 11. Offers, subscriptions, origin claims

- **ROSCA** (online negative options):
  - Clearly and conspicuously disclose all material terms **before** collecting billing info.
    That includes price, frequency, trial end and charges, and how to cancel.
  - Get express informed consent, e.g. an unchecked checkbox.
  - Provide a simple cancellation method.
  - The "Click-to-Cancel" rule was vacated by the 8th Circuit in July 2025. The FTC restarted
    rulemaking (ANPRM January 2026). ROSCA, Section 5 and state auto-renewal laws still apply.
    *[secondary]*
- **"Free" and "risk-free"**: disclose all conditions. "Free trial" with hidden shipping or
  auto-ship terms is deceptive.
- **Made in USA Labeling Rule (16 CFR 323)**: an unqualified claim requires final processing
  in the U.S., all significant processing in the U.S., and all or virtually all ingredients
  made and sourced in the U.S. Civil penalties apply. Use qualified claims such as "Made in
  USA with imported ingredients".
- **Earnings claims** (MLM or affiliate recruiting) are a separate FTC risk. Amare Global was
  sued in June 2026 over depression/anxiety/ADHD claims plus earnings claims. *[secondary]*

## 12. Enforcement context

- **April 2023:** FTC Notices of Penalty Offenses on substantiation went to about 670 health
  marketers, including supplement brands. Recipients face civil penalties (about $50k+ per
  violation) for unsubstantiated claims.
- **March 2025:** joint FDA and FTC warning letters to 10 companies over diabetes supplement
  claims. *[secondary]*
- FDA increasingly cites social posts, embedded video audio and infographics when they link to
  a cart. *[secondary]*
- **Hot categories:** diabetes or blood sugar, weight loss, kids' growth and ADHD, mood or
  anxiety, cognition or dementia, immunity or infection, cancer, heart, joints or arthritis,
  sexual enhancement, CBD/delta-8.

## 13. Quick red-flag word list (check context; not automatic)

Cure, treat, heal, prevent [disease], reverse, remedy, therapy, prescription-strength,
alternative to [drug], "doctors don't want you to know", "replaces your meds", antiviral,
antibiotic, antidepressant, anti-anxiety, painkiller, detox [disease], diabetes, blood sugar
control, insulin resistance, hypertension, lowers blood pressure or cholesterol, arthritis,
inflammation (when disease-tied), insomnia, depression, anxiety disorder, ADHD, Alzheimer's,
dementia, cancer, tumor, infection, cold or flu, COVID, IBS, leaky gut (as a disease), PCOS,
erectile dysfunction, "clinically proven" or "clinically studied" (needs matching evidence),
"doctor recommended", "#1", "guaranteed results", "FDA approved", "FDA registered",
"miracle", "breakthrough", "scientists agree", specific lbs or days.
