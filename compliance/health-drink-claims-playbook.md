# Health Drink Ad Claims Playbook (FDA / FTC / Meta)

Working reference for reviewing ad copy for a **functional health drink that is a conventional food/beverage — NOT a dietary supplement and NOT a drug.** Use it to (1) flag risky lines, (2) explain *why* a compliance reviewer flagged a line, and (3) propose safer rewrites.

> Not legal advice. Final calls belong to the brand's compliance/regulatory counsel. Researched Oct 2026; verify anything time-sensitive.

---

## 1. The three gatekeepers and what each one cares about

| Gatekeeper | Core question | What gets you in trouble |
|---|---|---|
| **FDA** (labels, website, and anything that shows "intended use") | *What is this product, legally?* | Claims make the product a **drug** (treat/cure/prevent/mitigate disease) or make it look like a **dietary supplement** (marketing a beverage as a supplement). Ads, social posts, and the website are all evidence of "intended use." |
| **FTC** (all advertising, incl. influencers and reviews) | *Is the claim true and backed up **before** it runs?* | Health claims without "competent and reliable scientific evidence" — generally RCTs in humans on the **actual product or equivalent formula/dose**. Misleading testimonials, undisclosed influencer ties, fake/incentivized reviews. |
| **Meta** (ad review) | *Does this ad break platform rules?* | Personal-attribute call-outs ("Are you…"), negative self-image, before/after for weight loss, clickbait, miracle/cure language, under-18 targeting. Meta is often **stricter** than the law. |

A line can be legal and still get rejected by Meta. It can also pass Meta and still be an FTC problem. Check all three.

## 2. FDA: the drug/supplement line for a conventional beverage

### 2a. Disease claim = drug claim (the bright line)
Any statement, express or implied, that the product **diagnoses, treats, cures, mitigates, or prevents a disease** makes it an unapproved drug, whatever is in the can.

FDA's disease-claim criteria (21 CFR 101.93(g), written for supplements but FDA applies the same line to foods). A claim is a disease claim if it implies an effect on:
1. A specific disease or class of diseases.
2. **Characteristic signs or symptoms** of a disease, in technical *or everyday* language (e.g. "joint pain from arthritis", "lowers blood sugar").
3. An abnormal condition tied to a natural state when it is uncommon or can cause significant/permanent harm (e.g. "severe depression after childbirth"). Common, mild conditions of natural states are OK (e.g. hot flashes associated with menopause, mild mood changes with menstrual cycle).
4. Disease via **name, formulation, citations, pictures/symbols** (e.g. product named "Gluco-Fix", an EKG graphic, citing a study about a disease population).
5. **Belonging to a class of products for disease**, like "antibiotic", "antidepressant", "laxative", "analgesic", "antiviral", "GLP-1 / like Ozempic".
6. **Substitute for a therapy or drug** ("ditch your meds", "natural alternative to HRT/Ozempic/Ambien").
7. **Augmenting a disease therapy** ("works with your metformin").
8. The body's **response to a disease or pathogen** ("supports immune response to flu").
9. Treating/preventing **side effects of disease therapy** when those are diseases.
10. Anything else that implies a disease effect.

"Disease" = damage to an organ/part/structure/system so it doesn't function properly.

### 2b. Structure/function claims for a *food* are narrower than for a supplement
- Supplements get broad structure/function claims (with the DSHEA disclaimer + FDA notification).
- **Conventional foods/beverages may make structure/function claims only when the effect derives from the food's _nutritive value_** (taste, aroma, nutrition: growth, essential nutrients, energy).
- Practical meaning: "Vitamin C supports immune function" (nutrient-driven) is safer than "Ashwagandha lowers cortisol" (a botanical, pharmacological effect). Effects that come from botanicals/actives *rather than nutrition* push the product toward looking like a supplement or a drug.
- FDA historically sent letters telling beverage makers (e.g. Tahitian Noni, 2010) that a product marketed as a beverage can't carry supplement-type claims. Enforcement here has been light, but the risk is real and a competitor/class-action plaintiff can use it.

### 2c. Don't look like a supplement or drug (packaging + vocabulary)
Avoid, in ads and on site:
- "Supplement", "Supplement Facts", "capsule/pill/tablet", "dose/dosage", "take 1 daily", "regimen", "clinical-strength", "prescription-strength", "Rx", "pharmaceutical-grade", "medicine/medicinal", "remedy", "treatment", "therapy", "protocol".
- Pill/pharmacy imagery, white-coat doctor "prescribing" the drink, syringes, pill bottles, before/after medical charts.
- Positioning as a substitute for meals/meds rather than a beverage people drink.

Prefer food/beverage language: "drink", "sip", "serving", "can/bottle", "flavor", "ingredients", "part of your daily routine", "made with…".

### 2d. Other FDA claim types (only if you meet the rule exactly)
- **Authorized health claims** (21 CFR 101.72–101.83) link a nutrient to *reduced risk of a disease* (e.g. sugar alcohols / "does not promote tooth decay", 101.80). These are allowed only with exact qualifying conditions and wording.
- **Qualified health claims** require an FDA enforcement-discretion letter and its exact wording.
- **Nutrient content claims** ("high in", "good source of", "low sugar", "healthy") have defined thresholds. "Healthy" has new criteria (final rule effective Feb 25, 2025; mandatory compliance Feb 25, 2028).

## 3. FTC: substantiation and endorsements

- **Standard:** Health claims need *competent and reliable scientific evidence*. Per the FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (Dec 2022, covers **foods and beverages**, not just supplements), that generally means **well-designed human RCTs**, statistically significant **and** clinically meaningful, on the product or an essentially equivalent formula/dose. Animal, in-vitro, and ingredient-only studies at different doses don't substantiate a product claim.
- **The claim is what the consumer takes away**, not just the literal words. Implied claims (via imagery, names, testimonials, "science-backed") need the same proof.
- **"Clinically proven / studied / doctor recommended / #1"** are establishment claims: you need exactly the level of proof stated.
- **Disclaimers don't cure** a deceptive net impression. "Results may vary" / "results not typical" do **not** fix an atypical testimonial. If a testimonial isn't typical, disclose what people **generally** achieve.
- **Testimonials count as claims by the brand.** If a customer says "it cured my insomnia", the brand made a disease claim, and an unsubstantiated one.
- **Influencers:** Disclose material connections clearly (and verbally in video). Brand and influencer are both liable for what the influencer says.
- **Consumer Reviews & Testimonials Rule** (effective Oct 21, 2024): bans fake reviews, AI-generated reviews, buying reviews that are conditioned on sentiment, insider reviews without disclosure, review suppression. Civil penalties per violation (~$51k+).
- **Notice of Penalty Offenses (Apr 2023):** ~670 health marketers (supplements, **functional foods**, OTC) were put on notice; recipients face civil penalties for unsubstantiated claims. Assume similar exposure.
- **Weight-loss "gut check" claims the FTC says are false on their face:** (1) lose 2+ lbs/week for a month+ without diet/exercise; (2) substantial loss no matter what you eat; (3) permanent loss even after stopping; (4) blocks fat/calorie absorption enabling substantial loss; (5) safely lose 3+ lbs/week for 4+ weeks; (6) substantial loss for **all** users; (7) loss from wearing/rubbing on skin.
- **Recent enforcement themes (2025–2026):** GLP-1-adjacent weight-loss marketing (e.g. NextMed: unsubstantiated "average 53 lbs" claims + fake reviews), employee/paid reviews (TruHeight), menopause products (Amberen: "restores hormonal balance", hot flash/night sweat/sleep relief claims — settled with a ban on unsubstantiated efficacy claims for any supplement, **food**, or drug). Truth in Advertising has sent letters to many menopause brands (Bonafide, Womaness, Happy Mammoth, GNC, etc.).

## 4. Meta ad policy (Advertising Standards → Health & Wellness, Personal Attributes)

- **Age:** dietary, health, weight-loss products → target **18+**. (General food/protein products are exempt, but a drink making weight/hormone/menopause claims will be treated as a health/weight product. Target 18+.)
- **No personal attributes:** can't assert or imply you know the viewer's health condition, body, or mental state. ✗ "Are you struggling with menopause?" ✗ "Your hormones are out of whack." ✗ "Tired of your belly fat?" → ✓ Talk about the product or people in third person ("Made for women navigating perimenopause").
- **No negative self-perception:** no statements of inferiority about appearance, body-shaming, "perfect body" ideals, pinching fat, close-ups of problem areas, exploiting insecurities.
- **No before/after side-by-sides for weight loss.**
- **No clickbait in health/weight context:** "Doctors hate this", "this one weird drink", "You won't believe…", shocking imagery.
- **No miracle/cure claims** for incurable diseases; no unrealistic or guaranteed results; time-to-results must be realistic.
- **Prescription drugs** (including GLP-1 references) fall under Drugs & Pharmaceuticals, which requires certification. Don't invoke them.
- **Lead forms** can't ask about health conditions without permission.
- **Data/tracking (since Jan 2025):** health & wellness advertisers may be restricted from lower-funnel optimization events and from sending some URL/parameter data. Avoid health-condition words in URLs, event names, and custom-audience names (e.g. `/menopause-relief`, `event=insomnia_quiz`), since these can trigger the restriction.
- Landing page must match the ad and must not carry claims the ad couldn't.

## 5. Benefit-by-benefit guide

Legend: 🟢 generally OK *if substantiated & nutrient-linked* · 🟡 gray, needs substantiation/careful framing · 🔴 drug/disease or platform violation

### Weight
- 🔴 "Treats obesity", "fat burner", "melts fat", "blocks fat/carbs", "lose 20 lbs in 30 days", "no diet or exercise needed", "natural GLP-1", "nature's Ozempic", "Ozempic alternative", "curbs cravings like a GLP-1", "suppresses appetite" (drug-like), "boosts metabolism by X%" (unless proven), before/after photos, "lose weight fast".
- 🟡 "Supports weight management", "helps you feel full" (needs fiber/protein data), "supports healthy metabolism" (nutrient-linked, e.g. B vitamins → energy metabolism), "low sugar swap for soda".
- 🟢 "Only 15 calories", "0 g added sugar", "a better-for-you swap", "fits into a balanced diet".

### Skin
- 🔴 "Clears acne", "treats eczema/psoriasis/rosacea", "heals", "anti-inflammatory for skin conditions", "reverses aging", "collagen-boosting results in 7 days" (unproven).
- 🟡 "Supports skin hydration/elasticity" (needs RCT on formula/dose), "helps reduce the appearance of fine lines" (needs proof).
- 🟢 "Made with vitamin C, which contributes to normal collagen formation" (nutrient-based), "beauty-from-within ingredients" (vague, low risk).

### Sleep
- 🔴 "Cures/treats insomnia", "sleep aid", "knock-out drink", "natural Ambien/melatonin alternative", "fixes sleep disorders", "sedative".
- 🟡 "Supports restful sleep", "helps with occasional sleeplessness" (classic supplement S/F wording; for a food, needs nutritive basis + proof), "helps you wind down".
- 🟢 "A caffeine-free evening ritual", "part of your wind-down routine".

### Energy
- 🔴 "Treats chronic fatigue / adrenal fatigue", "fixes fatigue from [condition]".
- 🟡 "Clean energy that lasts all day", "no crash" (needs proof), "boosts focus" (cognitive claim needs proof).
- 🟢 "With 80 mg of caffeine from green tea", "B vitamins that support energy metabolism" (nutrient-derived).

### Stress / mood
- 🔴 "Reduces anxiety", "treats depression", "antidepressant", "calms panic attacks", "lowers cortisol" (implies pharmacological effect; also a hormone claim), "balances your nervous system disorder".
- 🟡 "Helps you feel calm", "supports a relaxed mood", "helps manage occasional/everyday stress" (needs proof; avoid naming clinical conditions).
- 🟢 "A moment of calm in your day", "made with [ingredient] traditionally enjoyed in…" (careful: "traditional use" still implies efficacy if you pair it with a benefit).

### Menopause / perimenopause
- Menopause itself is a **natural state, not a disease**. Mild, common symptoms (hot flashes, mild mood changes) are not automatically disease claims for supplements. But **this is the hottest enforcement area** (FTC Amberen, TINA letters), and food products have narrower claim rights.
- 🔴 "Relieves menopause symptoms", "stops hot flashes/night sweats", "HRT alternative", "natural hormone replacement", "treats vaginal dryness", "prevents bone loss/osteoporosis", "restores hormonal balance", "boosts estrogen".
- 🟡 "Made for women in perimenopause and beyond", "supports you through every stage", "helps with occasional hot flashes" (needs RCT; supplement-style claim).
- 🟢 "Created with women in midlife in mind", "contains calcium & vitamin D" (nutrients, accurate amounts), "a ritual for the change".
- Meta: menopause effects ads must be 18+. Never "Are you going through menopause?"

### Hormones (general)
- 🔴 "Balances hormones", "fixes hormone imbalance", "boosts testosterone/estrogen", "lowers cortisol", "regulates insulin/blood sugar", "thyroid support for hypothyroidism", "PCOS support", "fertility booster".
- 🟡 "Supports a healthy hormonal cycle" / "supports hormonal wellness" (vague, still an implied efficacy claim that needs proof).
- 🟢 Talk about ingredients/nutrients and lifestyle, not hormone outcomes.

### Oral health
- Note: anticavity, antiplaque/antigingivitis are **OTC drug** categories, so these claims are drug claims for a beverage.
- 🔴 "Prevents cavities", "fights plaque/gingivitis", "heals gums", "kills bacteria that cause bad breath/decay", "remineralizes enamel", "treats dry mouth", "whitens teeth" (implies effect from a drink).
- 🟡 "Does not promote tooth decay" (only via the authorized health claim for sugar alcohols / non-cariogenic sweeteners under 21 CFR 101.80, with exact conditions), "supports oral microbiome" (needs proof; microbiome claims are scrutinized).
- 🟢 "Sugar-free", "freshens breath" (cosmetic, if it actually does), "won't leave sugar on your teeth like soda" (careful with comparison).

### Gut / immune / anything else that comes up
- 🔴 "Treats IBS/bloating disorder/leaky gut", "boosts immunity against colds/flu/COVID", "detoxes your liver", "antiviral", "anti-inflammatory" (disease-implying).
- 🟡 "Supports digestive health", "supports regularity" (fiber w/ amount), "supports immune function" (nutrient-based, e.g. vitamin C/zinc at meaningful %DV).

## 6. Red-flag words & patterns (quick scan list)

**Drug verbs:** treat, cure, heal, prevent, fix, reverse, eliminate, stop, relieve (symptoms), repair, remedy, combat/fight (a condition), protect against, kill (bacteria/virus).

**Disease/condition nouns:** any named disease or diagnosis — obesity, diabetes, blood sugar, insomnia, anxiety, depression, ADHD, PCOS, hypothyroid, IBS, arthritis, inflammation (as a condition), acne, eczema, osteoporosis, high blood pressure/cholesterol, gingivitis, cavities, infertility, ED.

**Drug-class / comparison:** GLP-1, Ozempic/Wegovy/Mounjaro, "like a prescription", HRT, antidepressant, sleeping pill, Rx, "pharmacist-formulated", "prescription-strength", "alternative to medication", "doctors are prescribing".

**Supplement signals:** supplement, capsule, dose, regimen, Supplement Facts, "take daily".

**FTC proof triggers:** clinically proven, studied, science-backed, doctor recommended, #1, guaranteed, X% / X lbs / in X days, permanent, works for everyone, instant, no diet/exercise, miracle, breakthrough, secret.

**Meta triggers:** "you/your" + body/health condition, "Are you…?", "Tired of your…", shaming, before/after weight, pinch/close-up of body area, "Doctors hate", "one weird trick", targeting under 18.

**Testimonial traps:** customer says it cured/treated/replaced meds; dramatic atypical results without generally-expected results disclosed; undisclosed free product/payment; reviews by staff/friends.

## 7. How to answer "Compliance flagged this — why?"

1. **Identify the net impression** — what a reasonable consumer would take away, including implied meaning from visuals, names, and context.
2. **Run the three lenses:**
   - FDA: Disease/symptom/drug-class/substitute? Or supplement-like? Is the effect beyond nutritive value?
   - FTC: Is it an efficacy/health claim? Is there RCT-level proof on *this* formula/dose? Quantified, "clinically", testimonial typicality, influencer disclosure?
   - Meta: Personal attributes, negative self-image, before/after, clickbait, unrealistic results, age?
3. **Name the specific trigger word(s)** and which rule they hit.
4. **Rate risk** 🔴/🟡/🟢 and say whether it's "illegal on its face" vs "OK only with substantiation" vs "platform-only issue."
5. **Offer 2–3 rewrites** moving down the ladder:
   - Disease → structure/function ("treats insomnia" → "supports restful sleep")
   - Structure/function → nutrient-anchored ("supports restful sleep" → "with magnesium, which contributes to normal muscle function")
   - Nutrient → experience/ritual/sensory ("your caffeine-free evening wind-down")
   - "You have X" → third person / product-centric.
6. **Note what proof would unlock the stronger claim** (e.g. "an RCT on the finished drink showing X would support 'helps you fall asleep faster'").

### Worked examples
| Flagged line | Why | Safer options |
|---|---|---|
| "The natural GLP-1 drink that crushes cravings." | FDA: drug-class comparison (criterion 5/6). FTC: implied drug-like weight-loss efficacy. Meta: Rx reference. | "Fiber-rich and filling, so it's an easy afternoon swap." / "Satisfying, low-sugar, and made to keep you going." |
| "Are you exhausted from menopause hot flashes?" | Meta personal attributes. FDA/FTC: symptom-relief claim needs RCT; menopause is a hot enforcement area. | "Made for women navigating midlife." / "A cooling, caffeine-free ritual for the change." |
| "Lower your cortisol and balance your hormones." | Hormone/physiological drug-like effect, beyond nutritive value. FTC Amberen precedent ("restores hormonal balance"). | "A calming ritual for busy days." / "Made with [ingredient] and magnesium." |
| "Customers lost 20 lbs in a month!" | FTC: atypical testimonial needs generally-expected results; gut-check claim. Meta: unrealistic result/time frame. | Drop the number; focus on taste/swap/ingredients. If using a testimonial, make it about taste/routine, not outcomes. |
| "Clinically proven to improve sleep." | Establishment claim: needs RCT on this exact drink. | If no study on this drink: "Made with ingredients studied for relaxation" (still 🟡) → "Your caffeine-free evening wind-down" (🟢). |
| "Fights cavities while you sip." | Anticavity = OTC drug claim. | "Sugar-free, so you're not sipping on sugar all day." |
| "Clears your acne from the inside out." | Disease claim + Meta personal attribute. | "Made with vitamin C & zinc. Beauty starts with what you drink." (nutrient amounts must be meaningful) |
